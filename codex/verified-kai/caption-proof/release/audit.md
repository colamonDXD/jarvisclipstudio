## 1. The paternity worry

- **0–3 seconds, original.** An NPC asks a group to move. The generic title and tight game crop give a stranger little reason to connect that instruction to Kai. Groq recognizes “no congregating”; an earlier output showed “no congregates.” The old live finisher accepted a YouTube word cache before listening to the source.
- **12–23 seconds.** Game characters and Kai speak over one another. Removing every plausible matching phrase can erase Kai. The revised OCR pass requires a close-in-time run of at least four consecutive words covering most of a high-confidence subtitle line. Uncertain speech stays. The title describes what Kai fears. The dialogue provides no paternity result.
- **Approximately 24–40 seconds.** Kai covers his face while the lower panel smears into colored shapes. At source 32 s, the original widescreen frame shows a pause menu with “Map” on the left and restart controls on the right. The background blur exists in the game. The center crop in the gaming layout hides the explanation. The development renderer can insert the source controls when it confirms a pause menu across multiple frames. [Uncropped source frame](../window_001-context.jpg)
- **42 seconds to the original ending.** The conversation turns to the car. The editorial cut holds through Kai’s concern and ends before that exchange. The two-layer composition still makes a viewer divide attention between the speaker and a partly static game screen.

## 2. The change in the water

- **0–12 seconds, original.** The seated character and controller prompts establish the scene. The old edit spends most of this time waiting. Groq’s full decode associates “Lapiz” with approximately 0–13 s. Offline Whisper instead places “my base” shortly before “whoa,” around 12–13 s. A 13-second word is visibly wrong even though both models produce similar word counts. The revised timing follows the corroborating short interval. The exact wording of that first utterance remains disputed.
- **19–26 seconds.** Kai says the character unlocked another dimension. The water and brightening game picture provide the context. The face and game remain simultaneous. A zoom on an arbitrary loud music note would take attention away from the change. The revised edit keeps this interval continuous. [Uncropped source frame](../window_002-context.jpg)
- **Around 31 seconds.** The old export shows stars for “damn.” The revised profanity filter keeps the word. The full-window decodes contain 29 and 30 words. A low count alone cannot establish missing speech in this music-heavy passage.
- **38–53 seconds.** Calling it a W gives the edit an ending. The later bag dialogue begins a different action. The editorial range is approximately 9.9–40.5 s of the source. Full-window playback is provided for judging all the speech. The character’s visual change is subtle even in the original footage, and this remains the weakest spectacle of the three.

## 3. The warning in the field

- **0–8 seconds, original.** The avatar walks and the opening is dominated by repeated calls. Groq’s full decode counts thirteen consecutive instances of “yo.” The offline decode hears six. A count-based confidence gate cannot resolve that disagreement. The full-window transcript preserves both readings.
- **16–32 seconds.** The character carries a body, then reaches a burning area with an item prompt. That is a more intelligible place to introduce the encounter. The editorial cut starts about 2.5 seconds before the object is named and retains the subsequent challenge. The clip stays over the existing 30-second minimum.
- **Around source 31 seconds.** At the exact moment both audio recognizers return “slur gun,” the on-screen prompt reads “FLASHER FLARE GUN.” The source label was independently detected in two nearby frames. The renderer uses that corroboration for the caption. Both raw transcripts remain available for comparison. [Original item prompt](../window_003_cold-context.jpg)
- **41–55 seconds.** An NPC spots the player and gives a warning. The old caption “our” conveys little on its own. The new captions group words and the cut holds the source dialogue and pursuit together. The lower panel briefly loses detail in the source. Later gameplay supplies a visual tail after the last spoken words. Whether that tail lands as a payoff is an editorial judgment.

## Rendering decisions

**Which cut I would publish first.** The father clip has the clearest complete thought. Kai states the fear, covers his face, and finishes the thought before the subject changes. The field clip is the strongest visual comparison: the item label and approaching character give the viewer something concrete to track. Its final pursuit is still open-ended. The water clip has a usable payoff in the spoken verdict, but its mostly seated opening is less legible without sound. I would put that clip third for a first-time viewer.

**Hook, pacing and framing.** The original father opening starts on a generic exchange; the revised title supplies the missing stakes. The game remains visible during Kai’s reaction. The water cut shortens the runway and keeps the change in the water continuous. The field cut removes the repeated calls before the item encounter. All three keep a single internal source timeline, preserving the relationship between the game sound, the event and the face. No measured replay score ranks the three chosen Kai moments against alternate moments from the same VOD.

**Transitions, zooms and sound.** The three review cuts use the source transitions and an uninterrupted dialogue mix. The evidence for these particular windows did not establish a frame-accurate reason for a new whip, speed ramp, impact or suspense bed. Source music is prominent in the water clip. Added effects would compete with an already ambiguous spoken opening. Compare the measured waveform lag at three points in each file and audition the old and new players separately.

**Captions and payoff.** The seam lettering measured 80 visible yellow pixels high in a 1080-wide father frame after calibration. The earlier development pass produced 42 pixels at the same timestamp. The title occupies a separate band. Read the two independent recognizers beside the burned words: agreement on a word count can coexist with a wrong word. The water payoff is the voiced approval, the father payoff is the expressed anxiety, and the field payoff is the confrontation. The quality of the final beat still needs a viewer judgment.

The normal live finisher now reads the audio of the actual source window, including a pre-existing teaser when present. It uses Groq turbo with the slang prompt, offline large-v3 and faster-whisper as available. Legacy discovery captions cannot win a cache lookup. YouTube json3 remains available after audio decoding fails. The cache key identifies the media bytes, decoder and prompt. Sparse coverage can select a stronger second decode. An excessive loss after OCR triggers a fresh pass. Emitted words are checked against the audio words inside the chosen edit.

The gaming renderer groups two or three words where speech permits. Upcoming words are white, the active word briefly scales to 115%, and spoken words stay yellow for the rest of the group. The preset uses Anton, a black stroke and shadow. Short gaps inside an utterance are bridged. Titles occupy a reserved top band. The first rendering attempt left black side margins. Visual review led to a smaller, width-filling treatment for the delivered versions.

The gaming edit keeps its internal source timeline continuous. It can trim the lead-in to a long acknowledgment and end on a spoken reaction followed by a pause. It avoids timed flash cuts, invented internal speed changes and beat-density repairs that would add a zoom to a quiet shot. A source reaction with suitable evidence can justify a ducked cue. For these proofs I supplied no independently verified event timestamp for a new sound effect. The game mix and streamer voice carry the scenes. No replacement music, unrelated GIFs, purchased stock or generated dialogue was used.

## Picking better source moments

The challenger adds optical flow, cut density, the 2–4 kHz share of audio energy weighted by loudness, local loudness changes against a two-minute baseline and timed exclamations. It combines that evidence with the existing voice score, then applies replay heat when available in production. The benchmark withholds replay heat from the inputs and uses it as the answer key. Timed text was available on three of the four sources. The Neon numbers use its saved same-source predictions from the original comparison.

Across the same four recordings the installed finder averaged **0.450**, the new combined finder **0.515**, and NeonClipping **0.574**. The challenger improved on the installed finder on three recordings. On the horror source it scored 0.392 against Neon’s 0.727. Both the activity experiment and the earlier context picker remain off by default. ## Tools and remaining limits

These changes reuse installed components. FFmpeg decodes, measures, crops and encodes locally. Its licensing depends on build options, with an LGPL base and optional GPL components. libass renders timed vector text locally on the CPU under its ISC license. OpenCV 4.x supplies CPU optical flow and access to source frames under Apache 2.0. Faster-whisper is MIT-licensed, supports CPU quantization and uses the existing CUDA runtime for large-v3 on this machine. Networked Groq is a hosted speech service. The activity experiment needs additional low-resolution video decoding and can take substantially longer than an audio-only scan.
